Data sources & method

Challenge resources (NASA Space Apps 2026 · all 5 used)

  • JPL Mars Trek: the main data source. Every orbital layer on the globe and map (Viking color mosaic, MOLA/HRSC/HiRISE/CTX elevation, THEMIS day and night IR, HiRISE images) was downloaded from Mars Trek's WMTS tiles and raw-DEM image services.
  • USGS-NASA Planetary Geologic Mapping Program: the Geology layer (map and globe). It shows all 1,311 polygons and 44 units of the Geologic Map of Mars (USGS SIM 3292, Tanaka et al. 2014) in the official colors. Hover a point for its unit, click for its description and interpretation.
  • Curiosity's proposed path up Mount Sharp: shown in the Curiosity panel ("NASA's planned route"), next to the real traverse on the map. The plan can be compared with what was actually driven.
  • NASA Worldview: the model for the interface. Like Worldview for Earth, the map switches between satellite layers of one place (image, elevation, thermal, geology) with a legend and source for each, and the opacity slider compares them. It is also a reminder that Mars, unlike Earth, has no daily global imagery: Mars layers are mosaics built over years.
  • Mars Perseverance Raw Images: white dots in the Perseverance view open the real raw image (Hazcam, Navcam or Mastcam-Z) taken on the sol of each event, from the gallery's own feed, with a link to the original. Curiosity has the same from the MSL raw-image archive.

Real NASA data shown

  • Whole-planet textures (globe and map background): Viking MDIM 2.1 color mosaic; MGS MOLA global DEM (128/64 ppd merge) colored by real height; Mars Odyssey THEMIS day and night IR global mosaics (THEMIS covers 65°S–65°N; the poles and thin orbit gaps have no data and stay dark. An empty block in the night overview tiles, 45°W–0° / 0–65°S, was rebuilt from the same mosaic's higher-zoom tiles). All via JPL Mars Trek. The 3D globe is rendered with three.js.
  • Elevation: MOLA + HRSC blended DEM (200 m, overview and Jezero); MRO HiRISE DEM of Gale (1 m, resampled); MRO HiRISE 1 m DEM of the Columbia Hills (Mars Trek DEM_1m_ColumbiaHills, Float32 values, resampled to ~10 m). Colors and the readout show heights in meters relative to the Mars datum.
  • Thermal: Mars Odyssey THEMIS day and night infrared mosaics (100 m). Brightness is relative surface temperature, not calibrated °C. At night rock and bedrock stay warm (bright) and dust and sand cool fast (dark).
  • Basic: MRO HiRISE (Curiosity and Spirit roving-site mosaics, controlled Jezero mosaic over CTX), Viking MDIM 2.1 color mosaic (overview). All via JPL Mars Trek.
  • Rover paths: Curiosity waypoints and traverse from the NASA/JPL MMGIS feed (to sol 5073). Spirit's corrected traverse from the PDS Geosciences Node MER Analyst's Notebook (sols 1–2209). Perseverance traverse and Ingenuity flight paths from the NASA/JPL MMGIS feed (to sol 2022).

Layer details (all measured data)

  • Elevation: contour lines and labelled heights are computed from the same DEMs as the colors (MOLA 2 km step globally, HRSC 1 km, HiRISE 100 m at Gale, CTX/HiRISE 10 m at Gusev, HRSC 50 m at Jezero). ▲/▼ mark the highest and lowest point of each area.
  • Thermal: real temperatures: daily ground (GTS) and air minimum/maximum from Curiosity REMS for 4,694 sols (sol 10–4995) and Perseverance MEDA air temperatures. The daily values come from NASA/JPL and Centro de Astrobiología (CAB, INTA-CSIC) outreach reports. Values below −110 °C or above +40 °C are dropped as sensor glitches. The science archive is in the PDS. Mini-TES results for Spirit come from Spanovich et al. 2006 (Icarus) and Fergason et al. 2006 (JGR). The MEDA night ground temperature comes from the EMIRS–rover comparison study (2022).
  • Thermal: inertia: MGS TES global thermal inertia (Mars Trek, 8 px/°). Values are read back from the published color scale (25–600 J m⁻² K⁻¹ s⁻½), so they are accurate to about ±5%. They match the published site values (Gale ≈ 370, Gusev ≈ 270, Jezero ≈ 290, dusty Tharsis < 100).
  • Geology: mineralogy: 4,572 aqueous-mineral detections in 5 classes (Ehlmann & Edwards 2014) and 1,648 hydrous-mineral detections (Carter et al. 2013), both from the Mars Trek feature services. Ground-truth mineralogy comes from Bish et al. 2013 (CheMin, Rocknest), Christensen et al. 2004 and Morris et al. 2006 (Spirit), and Liu et al. 2022, Beyssac et al. 2023, Udry et al. 2022 and Hurowitz et al. 2023 (Perseverance).

Resources layer (sulfur & concrete)

  • Positions: each marker sits at the rover's official position on the sol the measurement was made (NASA/JPL MMGIS waypoints for Curiosity and Perseverance; PDS MER Analyst's Notebook for Spirit).
  • Findings: NASA/JPL releases (Curiosity pure sulfur, Gediz Vallis, 2024; sulfate-bearing unit, 2022; Bagnold dunes, 2016); Blake et al. 2013 (Rocknest); Lane et al. 2008, Ming et al. 2006, Johnson et al. 2007, Wang et al. 2008 (Spirit sulfate soils); Williams et al. LPSC 2023, Stack Morgan et al. GSA 2022, Hurowitz et al. Goldschmidt 2023, NASA sample table and Simon et al. 2025 PNAS (Perseverance sulfates and regolith).
  • Sulfur concrete: Wan, Wendner & Cusatis 2016, Construction and Building Materials, "A novel material for in situ construction on Mars". Best mix: 50% sulfur + 50% regolith with grains ≤1 mm, >50 MPa.
  • Site ranking (Gale > Jezero > Gusev) is my own judgement from these data.

Radiation on each route

  • Dose per walk = time outside × the mean dose equivalent rate that Curiosity's Radiation Assessment Detector (RAD) measured on the surface in Gale crater: 0.64 ± 0.12 mSv/day (galactic cosmic rays, Aug 2012 – Jun 2013, ~300 sols). Source: Hassler et al. 2014, Science 343, 1244797, "Mars' surface radiation environment measured with the Mars Science Laboratory's Curiosity rover".
  • The same rate is used at Gusev and Jezero (RAD only measured in Gale; both sites are higher, so the real dose there is probably slightly higher). A spacesuit adds almost no shielding against cosmic rays. Solar particle events are not included: a large one would add a short spike, and crews would shelter in the lander.
  • For scale: NASA's career limit is 600 mSv effective dose (NASA-STD-3001 Vol. 1, OCHMO technical brief).

My predictions (not NASA's)

  • Crew lander zone: the cell with the lowest score for 400 m mean slope (DEM) + HiRISE surface roughness (craters, boulders) + elevation (lower means more air for braking). It must also sit 1.2–9 km from the target, depending on the site, so the plume does not hit heritage or science sites.
  • Routes: least-time path on the DEM (Tobler walking model, suited speed ~3 km/h on flat ground), with grades above 12–15° blocked; routes never enter DEM cells steeper than 15° (the red no-go hatching) and keep 50 m outside hazard zones. Rover time assumes 8 km/h.
  • EVA budget: walk out (walk_h) + work on site (1–4 h, chosen in the route panel, default 2 h) + walk back on the same path (back_h, same speed model, so a downhill walk out becomes an uphill walk back). A route "fits" when the total is within one ~8 h EVA: NASA's exploration suit requirement is EVAs of up to 8 hours (xEMU, NASA EVA-EXP-0075; Mars EVA suit target "8-hour EVA", NASA EVA technology roadmaps, ICES 2025). If it does not fit, the card gives the pressurised-rover option (8 km/h), where the suit is only worn on site.
  • Sites: picked from what the missions actually found (sulfur, sulfates, silica, soft soil, hardware) plus DEM analysis (north-facing slopes, highest point).
